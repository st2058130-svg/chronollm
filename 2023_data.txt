Year 2023 in Review

The Nobel Prizes of 2023 honored work that reshaped how science understands matter, medicine, and society. The physics prize went to Pierre Agostini, Ferenc Krausz, and Anne L’Huillier for methods that generate attosecond pulses of light—pulses lasting about one billionth of a billionth of a second, short enough to freeze electron motion inside atoms and molecules. Those techniques open paths in medical diagnostics and materials control. The chemistry prize recognized Moungi Bawendi, Louis Brus, and Aleksey Yekimov for discovering and synthesizing quantum dots, nanoparticles whose color and behavior depend on size. Quantum dots already appear in QLED screens and LED lighting and help surgeons illuminate tissue; researchers also see uses in sensors, thinner solar cells, and quantum communication. In physiology or medicine, Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman won for nucleoside base modifications that made effective mRNA vaccines against COVID-19 possible and deepened knowledge of how mRNA interacts with the immune system, with further promise for therapeutic proteins and some cancers. Jon Fosse of Norway received the literature prize for plays and prose that give voice to the unsayable. The peace prize went to Iranian rights advocate Narges Mohammadi for resisting oppression of women and promoting human rights. Claudia Goldin received the economics prize for advancing understanding of women’s labor-market outcomes across a century in which paid work for women rose sharply in many high-income countries.

Climate made 2023 unmistakable. Global average temperature reached about 1.45°C above the 1850–1900 baseline, the warmest year on record, and for a brief spell on 17–18 November daily global temperatures crossed 2°C above pre-industrial levels for the first time in the instrumental record. From June through December, monthly global averages repeatedly beat prior records by more than 0.2°C. Ocean heat content hit an all-time high, with roughly 464 zettajoules accumulated since 1960; oceans still absorb around nine-tenths of planetary heat gain. Antarctic sea ice set record lows for annual mean, summer minimum, and winter maximum, while Arctic September ice ranked among the lowest since 1979. Wildfires burned about 384 million hectares worldwide, the worst since 2017, and Canada alone lost some 15.5 million hectares in its most extreme modern fire season. Maui’s August fires were among the year’s deadliest wildfire disasters. Extreme rainfall struck Hong Kong, Myanmar, and Japan; heatwaves blanketed parts of South America for more than 150 days in places, and Phoenix recorded every July day above 43°C. Cryosphere assessments warned that ice-sheet melt could commit the planet to multi-metre sea-level rise over coming centuries if warming continues.

Space exploration delivered landmark landings and samples. On 23 August, India’s Chandrayaan-3 soft-landed near the Moon’s south pole, making India the fourth country to soft-land and the first to reach that region, and deployed the Pragyan rover. South Korea’s DANURI lunar orbiter began science operations early in the year, and Japan’s SLIM mission launched in September toward a precision landing. NASA’s OSIRIS-REx returned samples from asteroid Bennu in September—NASA’s first asteroid sample return—with evidence of carbon and water, while Psyche launched toward a metallic asteroid and ESA’s JUICE headed for Jupiter’s icy moons. Ingenuity continued flying on Mars; Curiosity and Perseverance remained active, even as planners struggled with a Mars Sample Return concept estimated near eleven billion dollars and judged unworkable in its then form. The James Webb Space Telescope kept revealing distant galaxies and chemistry in disks and atmospheres, including methane and carbon dioxide on exoplanet K2-18 b. On Earth-orbit and physics fronts, a Caltech-led experiment beamed microwave power from a satellite toward Earth, CERN confirmed antimatter falls under gravity like ordinary matter, China’s FAST telescope reported evidence for nanohertz gravitational waves, and JT-60SA in Japan achieved first plasma as a major tokamak step toward ITER-era fusion research. Shidaowan’s high-temperature gas-cooled reactor in Shandong entered commercial operation in December as a leading fourth-generation nuclear demonstration.

The world economy cooled unevenly. Global GDP growth ran near 2.7 percent, down from 2022, with developing economies still outpacing advanced ones. Merchandise exports fell about 4.3 percent to roughly 23.8 trillion dollars, while services exports rose about 8.3 percent to 7.9 trillion, lifted by travel and digital services. Foreign direct investment slipped about 2 percent overall even as least-developed countries saw FDI rise. Commodity indexes fell after 2022’s spike, with fuel prices down more than thirty percent. More than four-fifths of goods trade still moved by sea. In the United States, real GDP grew about 2.5 percent, unemployment averaged near 3.6 percent, consumer inflation cooled from 2022’s peak toward the mid-threes by December, and the federal funds rate ended in the 5.25–5.50 percent range after a long tightening cycle. The euro area and United Kingdom barely grew; China kept a large goods surplus and foreign-exchange reserves above 3.2 trillion dollars. Equity markets diverged: the S&P 500 and Nikkei posted strong gains while many European indexes lagged. Gold finished near 2,063 dollars an ounce.

Semiconductors and electric vehicles showed how uneven the technology cycle was. Worldwide chip revenue fell into the low five hundreds of billions of dollars, with memory collapsing hardest as phone, PC, and server demand softened. Intel regained the revenue lead even while shrinking; Nvidia entered the top tier on AI accelerators. Automotive chips gained share as modern cars packed thousands of semiconductors, and CHIPS Act–related private investment announcements in the United States climbed past 200 billion dollars. New energy vehicle sales approached 14.7 million units globally, with China taking nearly two-thirds. Power-battery shipments rose past 865 gigawatt-hours, led by CATL and BYD, while LFP chemistry remained concentrated in China and overcapacity in materials and cells became a central worry.

Military spending and conflict darkened the security picture. SIPRI estimated global military expenditure at about 2.44 trillion dollars, up 6.8 percent—the sharpest rise since 2009—with the United States, China, and Russia among the largest spenders. Armed conflicts remained active in dozens of states. The Russia–Ukraine war stayed high-intensity with little change on the front by year’s end. On 7 October, Hamas attacked Israel, killing about 1,200 people and taking hostages; Israel’s subsequent campaign in Gaza produced a severe humanitarian crisis and tens of thousands of Palestinian deaths by year-end estimates, alongside sharp rises in regional fatality totals. Sudan’s civil war erupted in April between rival military forces and became one of the year’s deadliest conflicts. Myanmar’s civil war and Sahel instability, including a coup in Niger, continued to drive displacement.

Natural disasters compounded suffering. EM-DAT recorded hundreds of hazard events, tens of thousands of deaths, and on the order of two hundred billion dollars in reported losses. The February earthquakes in Türkiye and Syria were the year’s deadliest and costliest single disaster complex, with more than fifty thousand deaths and tens of billions in damage. Storm Daniel’s September floods in Libya, including dam failures, killed thousands more. Major quakes also struck Morocco and Afghanistan. Hurricane Idalia hit Florida as a strong Category 3 storm; Hilary brought historic August rain to southern California; post-tropical Lee battered Atlantic Canada and New England with widespread outages.

Demography kept shifting south and into cities. The world population exceeded eight billion, with India and China each near 1.4 billion and Asia still holding roughly three-fifths of humanity. Developing economies housed most people and hosted most refugees. By late 2023, UNHCR figures put forcibly displaced people above one hundred million—on the order of one in seventy people on Earth—including tens of millions of internal displacees. Low- and middle-income countries hosted most refugees; children were overrepresented among the displaced. Asylum applications rose sharply, while returns and resettlement remained limited relative to need. Acute food insecurity stayed near record levels in conflict-affected countries, especially across parts of the Horn of Africa and Sudan, where drought, flood, and war overlapped.

Public health carried COVID’s long shadow even as some indicators improved. WHO statistics described hundreds of millions of years of life lost during the acute pandemic years, then noted reversed gains against malaria and tuberculosis and the rising share of deaths from noncommunicable disease. In the United States, registered deaths fell from 2022, and COVID-19 dropped from a top-four cause of death toward tenth as age-adjusted COVID death rates plunged. Heart disease and cancer remained the leading killers.

Science and medicine produced dense advances that are best told once, with their details intact. Researchers linked endogenous retroviruses and epigenetic aging, mapped more than three thousand human brain cell types across a major atlas, released a draft human pangenome, and described circadian “hands” of primary cilia on SCN neurons. GoT-Splice and related methods tied RNA splicing defects to blood disease biology. A mitochondrial-targeted nuclease approach showed promise against a common pathogenic mtDNA mutation in models. In November, UK regulators approved Casgevy, the first CRISPR-based therapy licensed for sickle-cell disease and transfusion-dependent beta thalassemia. Surgeons performed the first human whole-eye transplant—without restored vision in the grafted eye—and other teams explored cheek patches for peptide drugs and charted how pandemic isolation reshaped infant microbiomes. Materials and devices set records: silicon–perovskite tandem cells reached 33.9 percent efficiency, above the classic single-junction Shockley–Queisser limit; a 10-nanometer indium selenide transistor achieved high ballistic transport at low voltage; carbon-nanotube MOSFET processes scaled further; and photonic and superconducting platforms extended quantum coherence times. China’s selected advances highlighted Pangu Weather’s deep-learning forecasts, LHAASO’s extreme gamma-ray burst observations, nickelate superconductivity near liquid-nitrogen temperatures, the AT1 salt-alkali tolerance gene for staple crops, and commercial operation of the Shidaowan HTGR.

Culture and entertainment were dominated by a handful of shared moments. Beyoncé extended her Grammy record at the February ceremony, while Harry Styles, Lizzo, and others took major awards. At the Oscars in March, Everything Everywhere All at Once swept Best Picture, directing, and several acting prizes, with Michelle Yeoh becoming the first Asian woman to win Best Actress; Brendan Fraser won Best Actor for The Whale. On screens and in theaters, Barbie and Oppenheimer defined the summer “Barbenheimer” phenomenon, finishing as the year’s top two worldwide grossers near 1.4 billion and just under one billion dollars respectively, with The Super Mario Bros. Movie close behind. Paul Lynch won the Booker Prize for Prophet Song. On streaming, Miley Cyrus’s “Flowers” topped Spotify’s global year-end list. English Wikipedia’s most-viewed article was ChatGPT, followed by annual death lists, cricket tournaments, and the Oppenheimer and Barbie pages—an index of how AI, sport, and film shaped public attention. Google’s Year in Search likewise centered the Israel–Hamas war, the Titan submersible tragedy, and the Türkiye–Syria earthquake.

Labor unrest marked the American year. Hollywood writers struck for months over pay and AI-era residuals before a deal; actors later joined in a combined stoppage among the largest in decades. The United Auto Workers struck the Detroit Three in a coordinated campaign. Kaiser Permanente saw a historic healthcare walkout. Teamsters at UPS ratified a new contract after high-stakes talks. Together with casino, hotel, and other actions, hundreds of thousands of workers participated in stoppages—the busiest stretch since 2019.

Finance and housing reflected the rate shock. March’s U.S. regional banking crisis toppled Silicon Valley Bank, Signature Bank, and later First Republic after rapid deposit flight and mark-to-market losses on long-duration bonds; First Citizens acquired SVB’s deposits and loans under FDIC resolution. Silvergate wound down after crypto-linked deposit runs. Credit Suisse was absorbed by UBS in Europe. In U.S. housing, inventory edged up, homeownership held near 65 percent, median values stayed elevated, and rent growth cooled from the feverish 2021–2022 pace even as vacancy rates rose.

Diplomacy and domestic politics moved in parallel tracks. Saudi Arabia and Iran agreed in March, under Chinese mediation, to restore ties severed for years, helping a broader Middle East thaw that included Syria’s return to the Arab League. Finland joined NATO in April. BRICS invited new members including Saudi Arabia, Egypt, the UAE, Iran, and Ethiopia; the African Union joined the G20. In the United States, Kevin McCarthy’s speakership fight stretched into January, Brazilian capitol buildings were stormed by Bolsonaro supporters days after Lula’s inauguration, and later Mike Johnson became House speaker after McCarthy’s removal. Sports headlines included Max Verstappen’s Formula One dominance, South Africa’s Rugby World Cup title, Spain’s Women’s World Cup, Manchester City’s Champions League triumph, Inter Miami’s signing of Lionel Messi, and the Denver Nuggets’ first NBA championship. In January, Buffalo Bills safety Damar Hamlin suffered cardiac arrest on national television after a tackle, was revived on the field, recovered over months, and later advocated for wider AED access.

Taken together, 2023 was a year of record heat and ice loss, of AI entering everyday search and chat, of vaccine-era science recognized in Stockholm, of wars that multiplied displacement and hunger, of a banking scare inside a high-rate regime, and of cultural events that a large share of the planet watched at once. The numbers and names above are drawn once, in continuous form, so each major thread—climate, conflict, chips, vaccines, space, markets, and memory—can be read as a single story rather than a repeated list.
